# Why house rules exist, and how to write them for a new language

House rules decide **how a word form is turned into a knowledge key**.
The key is what carries the learner's progress across every text they
will ever read. Two texts that key the same word differently tear that
progress in half, silently: a word learned in text one shows up as new in
text seven, and nobody can see why.

So the rules are not style advice. They are the one thing that has to
stay the same for years.

## What has to be decided

**1. What is the base form, per part of speech?**

The question is harder than it looks the moment a language has no
infinitive (Bulgarian), several stems (Russian), or forms that look like
a different word (`vais`/`aller`). Pick one form, say why, and keep to
it.

**2. Where does a word end?**

Elisions, contractions, clitics, suffixed articles. `s'il` is two words,
`du` is one, `кучето` is one with an article inside it. Every language
draws this line somewhere else, and the line must be drawn once.

**3. How flat is the gloss?**

The gloss shows the *build* of the foreign language, not good target
language. Grammar hidden in an ending has to become visible - the
suffixed article, the case, the aspect. This is where a language needs
its own instruction, because what is hidden differs.

**4. What counts as a phrase?**

Fixed expressions whose meaning does not follow from the parts. Generous
enough to catch the real ones, strict enough that the text does not turn
into brackets.

## How to write one for a language nobody prepared

Take the default rules, then work through the four questions above with
one real paragraph in front of you. Write down every decision you had to
make - especially the ones you nearly made twice. That list *is* the
house rules.

## What does not belong here

- Anything about what a word description contains - that is
  `schemas/word-notes/`.
- Vocabulary, teaching order, level. The rules are about identity, not
  about learning.
