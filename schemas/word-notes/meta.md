# Why these recipes exist, and how to write one

A word description is what a learner sees on the word card. It is written
once, when the word first appears in any text, and it travels in every
package from then on. A description that is thin on the first encounter
stays thin.

So the recipe's job is: **make sure nothing important is missing, in a
language where "important" means something different than in the last
one.**

## What a recipe is not

Not a grammar of the language. Not everything true about a word. The
learner is reading a text and wants to know: what is this word, what
does it do, and what will trip me up.

Two or three labelled lines, then at most two sentences. If a recipe
grows past a screen, it has stopped helping.

## The three questions for a new language

**1. What does this language hide inside a word form?**

Every language buries something where a learner cannot see it. French
hides it in silent endings, Bulgarian in the definite article suffixed
to the noun, Russian in stress that nobody writes down. Whatever is
hidden must be spelled out - that is the whole point of the interlinear
layer, and the description continues it.

**2. Which stems must be learned from the start?**

This is the question that decides whether a learner can form words they
have never seen. A language with one stem per verb needs one line. A
language with two - a present stem and a past stem - needs both from the
first encounter, because learning one and meeting the other later feels
like learning the word twice.

Ask it for every part of speech, not only verbs: nouns with two plural
patterns, adjectives with a separate definite form.

**3. What is the pair that must always be named together?**

Some words are unlearnable alone: `ser`/`estar`, `por`/`para`, an
imperfective and its perfective twin, `ou`/`où`. When a language has such
pairs, the recipe says so, and the description names the partner every
time.

## Rules of thumb

- **A form the learner will meet belongs in the description.** A form
  they will not meet for two years does not.
- **Name what the word is confused with.** Homographs, near-homophones,
  false friends.
- **Say what is silent, irregular, or unpredictable** - never what is
  regular and obvious.
- **Examples carry their translation.** A preposition without examples is
  not learnable; an example the learner cannot read is not one either.

## Shape

    **Label** value · value
    **Label** value

    One or two sentences that do not fit a table.

Labels in bold, value on the same line, forms separated by `·`. A line
that the language does not have is left out - never "none".
