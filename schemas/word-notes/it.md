# What an Italian word description contains

Everything is written in the reader's own language; the examples are
Italian. Two or three **labelled lines**, then at most two sentences.

## The stems to keep in view

- the **present stem**, which often changes in the *io* form and keeps
  the change through the persons that carry the stress: *uscire → esco*,
  *venire → vengo*, *tenere → tengo*, *andare → vado*, *dovere → devo*.
- the **participio passato**, irregular in exactly the verbs a learner
  meets first: *fatto, detto, preso, messo, scritto, letto, visto,
  aperto, chiuso, rotto, rimasto, venuto*. Without it there is no past
  tense at all.
- the **auxiliary**, which is part of the verb and not a rule: *essere*
  or *avere*. With *essere* the participle agrees - *è andata*.

## Per part of speech

**VERB** — `Infinito`; the full `Presente`; `Passato prossimo` with its
auxiliary; `Participio` when irregular. Say which conjugation (-are,
-ere, -ire) and whether it takes *-isc-* (*finire → finisco*).

**NOUN** — `Singolare` with its article, `Plurale` with its article. The
article belongs to the word: *il, lo, l', la* and *i, gli, le* are chosen
by the sound that follows, and a learner who knows only *il* will get
*lo zaino* and *gli amici* wrong. Name irregular plurals (*l'uomo / gli
uomini*, *il braccio / le braccia*).

**ADJ** — the four forms, or the two when the adjective ends in *-e*.
Say when it goes before the noun, and when *bello* and *buono* shorten
(*bel libro, buon giorno*).

**ADV** — formation with *-mente*, and position.

**PRON** — the series: subject, direct, indirect, stressed, reflexive.
One example with its translation. Name the combined forms where they
occur (*me lo, glielo*).

**DET** — the full article series, and the *preposizioni articolate*
(*di + il = del*, *a + lo = allo*, *in + la = nella*).

**ADP** — one example per meaning with its translation, and the
articulated forms.

**NUM** — where the stress falls, and the elision in *ventuno*.

**CCONJ**, **SCONJ** — what it joins; whether it takes the subjunctive
(*benché, sebbene, affinché*).

**PART** — *ci* and *ne* are the ones that matter: what they replace,
with one example each.

**INTJ** — when it is said and how formal.

**PHRASE** — the literal words, then when it is used.

## Always

- **Double consonants are meaning**: *nono/nonno*, *casa/cassa*,
  *sete/sette*. Say so when the word has a near-twin.
- **Stress can distinguish words**: *àncora* (anchor) and *ancòra*
  (still), *prìncipi* and *princìpi*. Mark it where it is not on the
  second-to-last syllable.
- **Name the partner**: *essere/stare*, *sapere/conoscere*, *venire/
  andare*.
- Leave out: the meaning again, examples without a point, textbook prose.
