# What a French word description contains

One recipe per part of speech. Everything is written in the reader's own
language; the examples are French.

## Shape

Two or three **labelled lines**, then at most two sentences of remark:

```markdown
**Infinitif** chercher
**Présent** je cherche · tu cherches · il cherche · nous cherchons · vous cherchez · ils cherchent
**Passé composé** j'ai cherché

Regular -er verb. Takes its object without a preposition.
```

Labels in bold, the value on the same line, forms separated by `·`.

## The stems to keep in view

- the **present stem**, which changes in the persons that carry the
  stress: *venir → je viens / nous venons*, *boire → je bois / nous
  buvons*, *prendre → je prends / nous prenons*.
- the **future stem**, which is the infinitive for regular verbs and a
  different word for the common irregular ones: *aller → ir-*, *avoir →
  aur-*, *être → ser-*, *faire → fer-*, *pouvoir → pourr-*, *venir →
  viendr-*, *voir → verr-*, *vouloir → voudr-*. The conditional uses the
  same stem, so learning it once buys two tenses.
- the **participe passé**, together with its auxiliary. *être* is the one
  learners get wrong, and with *être* the participle agrees: *elle est
  allée*.
- the **imparfait stem**, which is simply the *nous* form without
  *-ons* - worth saying once, because it is the one thing in the French
  verb that is always regular (except *être*).

Name whichever of these is not predictable from the infinitive.

## Per part of speech

**NOUN** — gender shown through the definite article (`le livre`,
`la table`), the indefinite article, and the plural when it is not just
`-s`. Elision when the word begins with a vowel or mute h: `l'hôtel`.

**PROPN** — pronunciation only, and only when something is silent or
surprising. Usually no labelled line.

**VERB** — `Infinitif`, the full `Présent` (all six persons), and
`Passé composé` with its auxiliary. Say which group it belongs to
(-er, -ir, -re) or that it is irregular. Name `être` as the auxiliary
where it applies - that is what learners get wrong.

**AUX** — `être` and `avoir`: the full present, and the participle.

**ADJ** — `Masculin`, `Féminin`, `Pluriel`. Say when the adjective goes
before the noun, since most go after.

**ADV** — whether it is invariable, and where it stands relative to the
verb.

**PRON** — the series it belongs to (subject, direct object, indirect
object, stressed), one example sentence with its translation, and the
elided form when there is one.

**DET** — the series `masculin · féminin · pluriel`, and the elision or
contraction (`de` + `le` = `du`).

**ADP** — one example per meaning with its translation, and the
contractions with the article (`à` + `le` = `au`).

**NUM** — the liaison and whether a final consonant is spoken.

**CCONJ**, **SCONJ** — what it joins, one example, and whether it takes
the subjunctive.

**PART** — what it does, one example. For `ne`: which second part goes
with it.

**INTJ** — when it is said and how formal it is.

**PHRASE** — the literal words, then when it is used.

## Always

- Name **what the word is confused with**: `lit` (bed) and `il lit`
  (he reads), `ou` and `où`, `le` as article and as pronoun.
- Silent letters are worth a word when a learner would speak them.
- Leave out: the meaning again, examples without a point, textbook prose,
  etymology.
